Scheme Collapse Model: Formula Specification

Core formula, variables, defaults and data sources for the Urdu scheme calculator · Version 0.1 (demo) · 15 October 2026 · Engine file: formula.js
Status. This document specifies the model the calculator uses. The recruitment parameters (g, M, k, q) are placeholders that have not been calibrated against real collapsed schemes. Results below show how the model behaves. They are not findings. Until a methods note with calibration exists, do not describe the tool as "based on a paper".

1. What the model answers

The user gives three numbers: the amount they would put in, the promised return, and how often it pays. The model answers in three lines:

  1. When the scheme's money runs out (a calendar month, shown as "at the latest").
  2. How much a family joining now would lose.
  3. What the same money would earn in a bank or National Savings product. The calculator asks which bank only after lines 1 and 2 are shown, using a tile for each bank.

2. Core idea

  • A scheme promising r per month owes r times the money it holds, every month.
  • It has no real income. Payouts can only come from new members' deposits.
  • Any city or network has a limited number of people with money, so recruitment slows as the market fills.
  • The first month the cash on hand cannot cover what is owed is the collapse month.
  • The family's own amount does not change the collapse date. It only scales their loss and the bank comparison.

This is a simplified, step-by-step version of the Ponzi dynamics model in Artzrouni (2009). The differences are that this one runs in discrete monthly steps, recruitment is logistic, and payouts are cohort-based.

5. The monthly ledger

Seed (t = 0): E0 = 1,   P0 = 1 − k,   one cohort with B = 1, s = 0.

Then for each month t = 1, 2, 3, …

New money (logistic recruitment):   Dt = g · Et−1 · (1 − Et−1 / M) (2)
Money raised:   Et = Et−1 + Dt (3)
Pool after deposits:   P′t = Pt−1 + (1 − k) · Dt   and a new cohort opens with B = Dt, s = t (4)
Cash due:   Ot = (1 − q) · Σi owedi(t) (5)
Collapse test:   if P′t < Ot then C = t and stop (6)
Otherwise pay out:   Pt = P′t − Ot;   rolled-back money q · owedi is added to Bi (a matured lump-sum cohort restarts with Bi = q · owedi, si = t) (7)

Assumptions built into the ledger. New members are not owed anything in the month they join. Members never withdraw their principal from periodic schemes. Whatever is left in the pool at collapse goes with the operators. With g ≤ 1, equation (2) keeps Et ≤ M and Dt ≥ 0.

Accounting identity (checked on every run):   EC = cash paid out + k · EC + P′C (8)

6. Why collapse is certain: the tipping point

Ignore the pool for a moment. For a monthly scheme, fresh cash covers the month's dues only while (1 − k) · Dt ≥ rm · E. Substituting (2):

(1 − k) · g · (1 − E/M) ≥ rm
⇒   E* / M = 1 − rm / ((1 − k) · g) (9)

Before E*, the scheme takes in more than it pays and the pool grows. That growth is what makes it look healthy. After E*, every month pays out more than comes in, and the scheme lives on its saved pool until the pool runs out. If rm ≥ (1 − k)g, then E* = 0 and the scheme is underwater from the start. In plain terms: once recruitment growth falls below the promised return, collapse is close.

Because the model has no real business income, any positive return eventually collapses. That is why returns close to the bank rate get the SECP verdict (§10) instead of a date. At that level a real business could plausibly pay the return.

7. Where the family stands

Joining month:   J = a if the scheme's age is known; otherwise J = min { t : Et ≥ EC / 2 }  (where the typical rupee enters) (10)
Months left:   C − J  → Line 1 date = today + (C − J) months (11)
Cash received:   R = Σ A · p over months J+e, J+2e, … strictly before C   [lump sum: R = A(1+r) if J+e < C, else 0] (12)
Loss:   L = max(0, A − R)  → Line 2 (13)
Bank profit:   Π = A · b · (C − J) / 12  (simple profit, before tax)  → Line 3 (14)

The family is assumed to take every payout as cash. If the known age a ≥ C, the verdict is overdue.

8. Scheme-wide figures

Money lost:   Σi max(0, put ini − paidi) / Σi put ini (15)
Money behind:   Σi put ini [paidi < put ini] / Σi put ini   (share of money whose owners end up with less than they put in) (16)

The calculator shows "money behind" as "X out of every 10 people". This treats each rupee as one person, which is exact only if all deposits are the same size.

8a. Scheme types

Recruitment differs by kind of scheme. An app spreads faster than a neighbourhood committee, and doubling schemes keep going because members re-invest. The user picks a type first; it fills a typical promise and replaces the defaults in §3.2 with that type's settings. Only "money doubling" is anchored to a real case (Double Shah: doubling in about 70 days, ran about two years). The others are placeholder judgements until calibrated.

8c. Promise in rupees, warning signs, read-aloud

Rupees per period:   r = PRs / A    Lump sum, total back:   r = PRs / A − 1 (must be > 0) (4a)

The warning-sign check asks six yes/no questions (guaranteed profit, bonus for recruiting, no SECP or State Bank licence shown, business not explained, pressure to decide fast, delays on withdrawal). 0 yes: no big signs, still check SECP. 1–2: warning. 3 or more: high risk. It does not change the model; it gives a second, number-free check, which matters most when the model gives the SECP verdict. A "Listen" button reads the result lines aloud with the phone's own text-to-speech voice (Urdu needs an Urdu voice installed). A chart shows the scheme's cash pool Pt from start to collapse, with "today" marked.

14. Limitations and calibration plan

  1. Not calibrated. g, M, k, q are placeholders. The plan is to fit them to 3–5 collapsed schemes with a known promise and lifespan (Double Shah, the Modaraba scam, Pakistani crypto-app collapses) and to report the fit.
  2. "At the latest" is conditional. Operators often run before the money runs out, which makes the real date earlier. Reinvestment (q > 0) makes the model date later. A date computed with q = 0 is therefore not a guaranteed upper bound.
  3. No withdrawals or bank runs. Members pulling out principal, or a panic, would bring collapse earlier.
  4. No real income. The model treats every payout as coming from new deposits. That is why returns near the bank rate get no date.
  5. Typical joiner. Joining at the half-money point is a default for users who don't know the scheme's age. Late joiners lose much more than this figure.
  6. Monthly steps. Daily and weekly payouts are summed by month. Lump-sum periods are rounded up to whole months.
  7. Bank rates go stale. Rates are embedded in the offline file and must be refreshed and rebuilt periodically. The file shows the date the rates were checked.
